# Advanced: Service Recovery & De-escalation

*This is an upskilling module — it unlocks once all core training is completed. It builds on the Customer Service & Brand Standards module.*

## What service recovery means

Service recovery is what you do after something has already gone wrong — a late delivery, a stock-out on a promised item, a billing mistake. Done well, a good recovery can leave a customer trusting the brand *more* than if nothing had gone wrong at all, because it proves what happens when things don't go to plan.

## The LAST framework

- **Listen** — fully, without interrupting or getting defensive, even if the complaint feels unfair.
- **Apologize** — genuinely, for the impact on them, not a scripted line. ("I'm sorry this has cost you time" vs. a flat "sorry for any inconvenience.")
- **Solve** — offer a concrete fix within your authority; if it requires more, get someone who has that authority involved immediately rather than leaving the customer waiting.
- **Thank** — thank them for raising it. Most unhappy customers don't complain — they just leave and don't come back. The ones who tell you are giving you a chance to fix it.

## De-escalating a heated situation

1. **Lower your voice and slow your pace** — matching someone's heightened energy escalates it further; a calm, steady tone is contagious in the other direction too.
2. **Don't take it personally, even when it's aimed at you.** You're usually standing in for a frustration with the situation, not actually the target.
3. **Give them room to vent before redirecting** — cutting someone off mid-frustration often makes them repeat themselves louder.
4. **Use their name and specific details** from what they said — it signals you're actually listening, not running a script.
5. **Know when to bring in a manager** — not as a failure on your part, but because some situations need someone with more authority to resolve, and recognizing that early is itself a skill.

## When a customer is being unreasonable or abusive

De-escalation has limits. You are not required to accept abuse to "win back" a customer. If someone becomes abusive, it's appropriate to calmly set a boundary ("I want to help, but I need us to speak to each other respectfully") and to involve a manager or end the interaction if it continues.

## Turning a recovery into loyalty

The goal isn't just to close the complaint — it's to make the customer feel like a person, not a ticket. A small, genuine gesture (a sincere apology plus a concrete fix, delivered without defensiveness) often builds more loyalty than a transaction that went perfectly but felt impersonal.
