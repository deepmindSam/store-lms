# Customer Service & Brand Standards

## Why we do what we do

Every interaction either builds trust in the brand or chips away at it. Customers rarely remember exact prices, but they remember how they were treated — especially when something went wrong and was handled well. Consistent service is what lets customers walk into any of our stores and know what to expect.

## The standard, in one line

Be genuinely helpful, make it easy, and own the problem if something goes wrong — don't make the customer work to get good service.

## Core behaviors

1. **Greet with intent, not a script.** A warm, specific greeting beats a robotic one. Notice what the customer actually needs rather than reciting a line.
2. **Listen before solving.** Let the customer finish explaining before jumping to a fix — half of complaints are really about being heard, not just the issue itself.
3. **Never say "that's not my department" as a dead end.** If you can't solve it, find who can and hand it off properly — walk them to the right person or follow up yourself, rather than pointing and leaving them to figure it out.
4. **Own mistakes without excuses.** "We got that wrong, here's how I'll fix it" lands far better than explaining why it happened. Save the explanation for after you've addressed the problem, and only if they want it.
5. **Match energy to the moment.** Quick and efficient for someone in a rush; patient and thorough for someone who needs more help (older customers, someone less familiar with the product, a parent managing kids while shopping).
6. **Represent the brand in appearance and tone**, consistent with your store's dress code and communication style — this is part of what makes the brand recognizable and trustworthy across every location.

## Handling a complaint or an upset customer

1. Let them finish. Don't interrupt to defend the store.
2. Acknowledge how they feel before jumping to policy ("I can see why that's frustrating" before explaining the return window).
3. Solve what's within your authority; escalate what isn't — don't leave them with "there's nothing I can do" if a manager could actually help.
4. Follow up if you promised to — a dropped promise is worse than not promising at all.

## What "good service" is not

- Agreeing to anything just to end an uncomfortable conversation (that's how policy exceptions spiral and become unfair to other customers).
- Being so fast that the customer feels rushed or unheard.
- Being polite but unhelpful — courtesy without actually solving the problem isn't service.

## The test

After an interaction, ask yourself: *did this customer leave trusting us more or less than when they walked in?* That's the real measure, more than whether the transaction technically went fine.
