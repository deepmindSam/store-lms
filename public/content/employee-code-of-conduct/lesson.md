## Why we have a code of conduct

A code of conduct exists so that everyone — regardless of store, shift, or role — operates to the same standard. It protects customers, protects colleagues, and protects the business's reputation. "We've always done it this way" is not a defense if it conflicts with the code.

## Core principles

1. **Honesty and integrity.** Don't falsify records — sales figures, stock counts, timesheets, expense claims, or incident reports. A small lie to avoid an awkward conversation becomes a serious issue once discovered.
2. **Treat people fairly.** Customers and colleagues are treated with respect regardless of appearance, accent, disability, age, background, or how they're dressed. No favoritism in scheduling, discounts, or promotions based on personal relationships.
3. **Protect company and customer property.** Don't use company resources (till, stock, delivery vehicles, systems) for personal benefit. Theft — including "borrowing" stock or cash — is gross misconduct.
4. **Follow safety and legal requirements.** Age-restricted sales (alcohol, tobacco, lottery, etc. where applicable), health and safety procedures, and licensing conditions are not optional or negotiable, even under pressure from a customer or a busy shift.
5. **Act within your authority.** Don't make promises to customers, suppliers, or colleagues that you don't have the authority to keep (refunds beyond policy, schedule changes, pricing exceptions) — escalate to a manager.
6. **Speak up.** If you see a breach of this code — by anyone, at any level — report it. Retaliation against someone who raises a genuine concern in good faith is itself a serious violation.
7. **Represent the brand consistently.** What you say and do while identifiable as an employee (in uniform, on shift, or posting about work on social media) reflects on the whole business.

## What happens when the code is broken

Breaches are taken seriously and handled consistently, regardless of seniority or how long someone has worked here. Minor, first-time issues are usually coaching conversations; dishonesty, theft, discrimination, harassment, or safety violations are treated as serious or gross misconduct. If you're ever unsure whether something is acceptable, ask your manager before doing it, not after.
